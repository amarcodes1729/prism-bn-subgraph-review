Human activity can alter biophysical environments, ecosystems and natural resources on a scale that leads to environmental degradation, ecological crisis and, in extreme cases, ecological collapse. Although anthropogenic pollution has existed since the Agricultural Revolution, modern pressures are closely associated with population growth, rapid economic growth and neoliberal economic policies. These societal forces are linked to overconsumption and overexploitation, expanded fossil-fuel use and extensive changes in land use.

Fossil fuels are burned for electricity generation, car and plane travel, space heating and manufacturing. These activities contribute to pollution from human activity and are associated with global warming, a heated environment and ocean acidification. At the same time, societal pressures encourage modification of the built environment, deforestation and broader forest destruction. Together with overconsumption, overexploitation and anthropogenic pollution, these land-use changes reshape ecosystems and the resources on which they depend.

Such environmental alterations are connected not only to climate and ocean impacts but also to biodiversity loss and mass extinction. Global warming, wider environmental heating and ocean acidification further intensify pressures on biodiversity and are associated with environmental degradation, ecological crisis and possible ecological collapse.

The consequences ultimately extend to humanity itself. Climate and ocean disruption can create catastrophic risks to the survival of the human species, including survival risks specifically linked to global warming. Biodiversity loss and mass extinction provide another pathway to danger, creating distinct risks to human survival as ecological systems deteriorate.
