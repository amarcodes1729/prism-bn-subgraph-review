Human activity can alter biophysical environments, ecosystems and natural resources on a broad scale. Major societal pressures include population growth, neoliberal economic policies and rapid economic growth. These pressures are associated with overconsumption and overexploitation, as well as land-use changes such as modification of the built environment, deforestation and forest destruction.

The same societal drivers are linked to fossil-fuel combustion for electricity generation, car and plane travel, space heating and manufacturing. Such combustion contributes to pollution from human activity, part of an anthropogenic pollution context extending back to the Agricultural Revolution. Fossil-fuel use is also associated with global warming, a heated environment and ocean acidification.

Pollution, intensive consumption and changing land use all contribute to environmental alteration. Changes to environments, ecosystems and natural resources are in turn linked to climate and ocean impacts, biodiversity loss and wider environmental degradation. Climate-related warming and ocean acidification further threaten biodiversity, with the possible outcome of mass extinction, while both direct environmental alteration and climate impacts can deepen an ecological crisis and raise the prospect of ecological collapse.

At the most severe level, these interconnected pressures pose risks to the survival of the human species. Global warming can create such a risk directly, while biodiversity loss and mass extinction provide another pathway. Together, climate disruption, ocean change and declining biodiversity can therefore escalate environmental degradation into a catastrophic threat to human survival.
