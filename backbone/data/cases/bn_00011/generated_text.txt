Climate change can affect mental health through direct exposure to extreme weather, disruption of social and economic life, awareness of long-term threats, and changes to the natural environment. Heat extremes and other extreme weather events are associated with exposure to heat, humidity, heavy rainfall, drought, wildfires, and floods. These hazards can disrupt economic and social activity and reduce farmland food production, while also contributing to psychological distress.

The resulting mental health conditions can include post-traumatic stress disorder and other stress-related conditions, as well as eco-anxiety, ecological grief, eco-anger, and anxiety about the future of younger generations. The particular response is also related to the kind of exposure and disruption involved. Floods and wildfires, for example, are more closely associated with post-traumatic stress than less acute exposures, while drought is strongly linked with reduced farmland food production. Concern about climate change and future generations’ quality of life can itself be associated with eco-anxiety and future-generation anxiety, although such awareness may also encourage adaptive action.

These effects are not distributed equally. Environmental injustice and economic inequality shape vulnerability and access to protective environments. People with pre-existing serious mental illness, Indigenous peoples, migrants and refugees, and children and adolescents may face particular risks, and their circumstances are associated with differing patterns of stress-related conditions, ecological grief, and anxiety about the future. Economic or social disruption and declining food production can further compound these pressures.

Mental health consequences can place an increased burden on healthcare systems and raise demand for psychiatric services. They may also be reflected in psychiatric and other mental health-related hospital admissions, self-harm rates, suicide rates, and suicidality. At their most severe, climate-related mental health problems are associated with increased mortality, including suicide mortality, as well as reduced mental wellbeing.

Access to nature forms another important part of this relationship. Green and blue natural spaces are associated with beneficial mental health effects when they remain intact and accessible. By contrast, freshwater pollution, deforestation, and landscape degradation are linked to reduced or unequal public access and to poorer wellbeing. Social inequality can reinforce this pattern by limiting access even where natural spaces remain available. The mental-health implications of climate change therefore extend beyond weather hazards alone, encompassing social vulnerability, environmental degradation, healthcare demand, mortality, and the unequal availability of environments that can support wellbeing.
