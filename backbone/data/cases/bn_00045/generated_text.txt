Islamic attitudes toward poverty and asceticism developed through the interaction of religious teaching, historical change and differing scholarly traditions. The Hijra, the flight from Mecca, and the subsequent post-Hijra transformation are associated with a shift from a primarily charitable religious message toward a movement with a stronger political and conquest-oriented character. Asceticism later reached a peak during the Middle Ages.

These changes shaped attitudes toward material wealth. A charitable religious outlook was closely associated with sharing wealth with the poor, whereas political and conquest-oriented forms of the movement were more compatible with the enjoyment of worldly goods. At the doctrinal level, the Quranic admonition against forbidding good things supported such enjoyment, while a message centered on the poor encouraged sharing. A belief in the necessity of sacrificing worldly possessions instead favored renunciation and the abandonment of wealth.

Sufism, Sunni scholarship and Shi’ite scholarship treated these questions differently. Sufism was particularly associated with ascetic practices such as voluntary poverty, self-denial, abandonment of material wealth and full-time worship of God. Sunni and Shi’ite scholarship were more strongly linked to the Quranic warning against prohibiting good things and were correspondingly less likely to endorse those practices.

Scholarly treatment also depended on the form that asceticism took. Voluntary poverty could prompt encouragement of renunciation, while self-denial was more often rejected. The abandonment of material wealth, including as part of a life devoted fully to worship, was especially likely to be venerated. These responses produced contrasting understandings of poverty: its relationship with voluntary poverty could be described as tenuous, but poverty could also be idealized, regarded as spiritually virtuous, or discussed more broadly through Islamic ideas about its nature.
