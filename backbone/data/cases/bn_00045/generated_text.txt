Islamic ideas about poverty developed through a tension between ascetic renunciation and the legitimate enjoyment of worldly goods. Poverty could be idealized as spiritually virtuous, yet its relationship with voluntary poverty remained tenuous. The relevant practices ranged from self-denial and the abandonment of material wealth to sacrificing worldly possessions and devoting oneself full-time to the worship of God.

Historical changes shaped this tension. The Hijra, the flight from Mecca, and the post-Hijra transformation were associated with an increasingly political and conquest-oriented character, while the movement also retained a charitable religious message. These different emphases corresponded to different attitudes toward wealth: the charitable message was most closely associated with sharing possessions with the poor, whereas political and conquest-oriented interpretations were more compatible with the enjoyment of worldly goods. Asceticism later reached a peak in the Middle Ages.

Doctrinal arguments also varied among Sufism and Sunni and Shi'ite scholarship. One line of reasoning drew on the Quranic admonition against forbidding the good things that are available to believers, supporting the enjoyment rather than the renunciation of worldly goods. Other interpretations emphasized a message of sharing with the poor or maintained that worldly possessions had to be sacrificed. Such justifications informed attitudes ranging from renunciation and charitable sharing to outright abandonment of material wealth.

These religious traditions and attitudes toward wealth, in turn, framed ascetic practice. Sufism had the stronger association with voluntary poverty, self-denial, abandonment of wealth, and full-time worship, while Sunni and Shi'ite scholarship were less closely associated with those practices. Renunciation and sacrifice were linked to ascetic conduct, whereas enjoyment of worldly goods generally stood apart from it.

Scholarly treatment reflected the particular practice under discussion. Voluntary poverty could prompt encouragement of renunciation; self-denial was more often rejected; and abandonment of material wealth or full-time worship was commonly treated with veneration. These responses ultimately shaped whether poverty was presented as an ideal, regarded as spiritually virtuous, treated as part of broader Islamic thinking about poverty, or understood as having only a tenuous connection to voluntary deprivation.
