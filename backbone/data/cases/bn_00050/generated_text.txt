Period poverty encompasses inadequate access to menstrual products, education, and the facilities needed to manage menstruation safely. It is closely associated with broader structural issues of gender inequality and economic injustice, which shape access to sanitary products, menstrual-hygiene education, safe water, sanitation, washing facilities, and waste-management services.

Policy responses, including period-poverty legislation, address these barriers by supporting access to proper menstrual products, the education needed to use them effectively, and suitable hygiene infrastructure. Such legislation is influenced both by recognition of structural injustice and by public perceptions of period poverty. Those perceptions, in turn, may be shaped through social-media advocacy, consciousness raising, public-awareness campaigns, and arts advocacy.

Safe period management depends on more than the availability of products. A lack of menstrual-hygiene education, washing facilities, waste management, safe water, or sanitation can make effective management difficult even when some sanitary products are available. Fear of shame and menstrual stigma create an additional barrier. Together, inadequate resources, limited knowledge, deficient infrastructure, and shame-related pressures are associated with unsafe period management.

The consequences extend across daily participation, physical health, and psychological well-being. Unsafe menstrual management is linked to missed school and work days, as well as infections and reproductive-tract complications. It can also be associated with negative psychological consequences, including shame. By contrast, access to appropriate products, practical education, and safe hygiene facilities supports safer period management and addresses period poverty as both a public-health concern and an issue of gender and economic justice.
