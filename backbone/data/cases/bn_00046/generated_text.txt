Galicia’s agrarian society was marked by severe and widespread poverty. Population growth and the division of the countryside into small peasant plots intensified social deprivation, while landowners and the government neglected institutional reform. Inadequate education and primitive agricultural techniques further constrained farming capacity, contributing to reduced or merely subsistence-level productivity.

These conditions were intertwined with chronic malnutrition, famine, and disease. Food and health crises undermined economic output, while low productivity in turn accompanied extreme poverty and regularly recurring famines. The combined pressure of limited land, weak agricultural capacity, poor output, and institutional inaction left much of the population living close to subsistence.

The resulting hardship also shaped Galicia’s public image. Chronic malnutrition, famine, and disease contributed directly to outside perceptions, while extreme and widespread poverty reinforced them. Galician misery became proverbial; the province acquired a mocking nickname and was compared with British Ireland as a symbol of enduring rural deprivation.
