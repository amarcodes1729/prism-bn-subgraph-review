The COVID-19 pandemic, lockdowns and related precautions were associated with a worldwide economic disruption that ranged from slowdown and stagnation to recession and broader economic crisis. Financial markets initially experienced a stock market crash, followed in some cases by recovering indexes and record highs. The wider economy also moved toward recovery, though the pace varied and output could remain below pre-pandemic levels, return to those levels or eventually exceed them.

These macroeconomic conditions shaped demand. During the downturn, consumer activity stagnated, contributing to severe losses in tourism and hospitality and to a broader consumer-activity downturn. As recovery developed, global demand surged, with strong Asian energy demand forming part of the renewed pressure on commodity markets. The energy industry itself was also vulnerable to collapse amid the interaction of demand conditions, economic weakness and commodity-market disruption.

Oil and energy markets were affected by both economic conditions and geopolitical shocks. The Russia–Saudi Arabia oil price war was associated with an oil price war and a sharp oil-price drop, while the escalation of the Russo-Ukrainian War and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine were linked to a global energy crisis. Shifting demand likewise influenced whether commodity markets faced falling oil prices or a broader energy shortage, and these shocks carried consequences for energy and consumer-facing sectors.

The labor-market effects of slowdown, stagnation, crisis, recession and depression included rapid increases in unemployment, mass unemployment claims, losses in working hours around the world and unemployment reaching 10 percent in some settings. The wave of claims placed public-benefit administration under acute strain, overloading unemployment-claims processes and the computer systems used to administer unemployment insurance.

The downturn also created particular hardship in developing countries, including declines in remittances and COVID-19-related famine. Across economies, the depth of the macroeconomic contraction was reflected in whether gross domestic product remained below its pre-pandemic level. By contrast, economic recovery was associated with GDP returning to or surpassing that benchmark, renewed global and Asian energy demand, and improved sectoral activity, even as the later global energy crisis demonstrated that recovery could bring new commodity pressures rather than a simple return to stability.
