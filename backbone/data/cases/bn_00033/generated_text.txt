Technological unemployment describes joblessness associated with innovations that reduce or replace human labor. Its effects may take the form of worker displacement, losses concentrated in a particular profession or industry, or broader labor-market disruption. These outcomes are also classified as structural unemployment when technological change alters the kinds of work available and the skills required.

The debate began with labor-saving mechanical machines and remains relevant to mechanical-mind automation, advanced artificial intelligence, and advanced robotics. Mechanized looms affected artisan weaving and artisan weavers, provoking Luddite protests. More recent changes such as self-service tills and cashierless stores have placed retail cashiering and retail cashiers at the center of similar concerns. AI and robotics broaden the discussion to intellectual and physical tasks, including the possibility that all tasks could eventually be affected.

Such technologies can minimize the human role in production, leave it necessary, or create roles in which people complement AI. The resulting relationship between people and machines shapes whether automation displaces workers, eliminates jobs within particular professions or industries, or instead creates employment through innovation. Employment outcomes can therefore range from short-term job losses and lasting increases in unemployment to mass unemployment or even an asserted end of employment. An alternative view is that innovation produces new jobs on balance.

Technological change may also restructure the economy by creating new industries and new jobs. This possibility underlies the argument that displacement is a temporary maladjustment rather than a permanent decline in employment. By contrast, all-tasks-automation assessments emphasize the prospect of lasting unemployment, while human-complementarity assessments stress the continuing or newly configured role of people in production. Jobs-on-balance assessments focus on whether innovation ultimately creates at least as much work as it removes.

These competing assessments influence how technological unemployment is understood and how society might respond. Limited or short-lived losses invite adjustment-oriented interpretations, whereas mass unemployment or an end of employment raises more fundamental questions about income and participation in economic life. Responses have ranged from protests by displaced workers to proposals for a basic income for non-workers. The central dispute is thus not simply whether machines can perform human tasks, but whether technological disruption remains temporary, becomes lasting, or is offset by new industries and forms of work.
