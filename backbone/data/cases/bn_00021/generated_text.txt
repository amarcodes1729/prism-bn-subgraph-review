The roots of the later United States–Soviet rivalry reached back to the 1917 Russian Revolution. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, under which Soviet Russia ceded territories to Germany, and Allied intervention in the Russian Civil War contributed to an early breakdown in relations. The contrast between Soviet communism and Western capitalist democracy, together with support for a democratic Europe, sustained mutual suspicion and repeatedly strained Western–Soviet cooperation.

During the Second World War, the United States and the Soviet Union nevertheless joined in a wartime alliance. The Allied victory and defeat of Nazi Germany brought the liberation of Eastern Europe from Nazi control, but the resulting military situation created new disputes. The Red Army remained present across Eastern Europe, while Western access to Berlin became a central concern. Soviet control of Eastern Europe and the broader division of Europe placed further pressure on the former alliance.

The political future of liberated territories became inseparable from ideology and military presence. The Soviet Union established communist regimes in areas under its influence, whereas Western governments preferred democratic governments. In the West, these developments were viewed as Soviet expansionism and the spread of communism. From the Soviet perspective, Western policies could instead appear to represent a United States effort to impose its own influence in Europe.

Economic reconstruction deepened the division. The Marshall Plan pursued European financial reconstruction, the stabilization of capitalist economies, and the provision of American aid to Europe. The Soviet Union rejected the Marshall Plan and established Comecon to promote economic cooperation among communist states. The two initiatives therefore became part of the wider contest over influence and reinforced opposing strategic perceptions.

Berlin brought the confrontation into especially sharp focus. The Berlin Blockade and the Western response through the Berlin Airlift intensified diplomatic and military tensions, bringing the opposing sides to the brink of direct military conflict. Alongside disagreements over Eastern Europe and competing economic programs, the crisis transformed strained cooperation and mutual suspicion into an increasingly explicit geopolitical confrontation.

These pressures solidified the Cold War. The mounting conflict led to the creation of NATO and the formalization of Western Bloc military alliances, confirming that the wartime partnership had given way to an enduring US–Soviet rivalry and a divided Europe.
