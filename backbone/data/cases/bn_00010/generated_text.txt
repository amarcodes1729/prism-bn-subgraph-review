Morocco has a developing market economy that has moved away from extensive reliance on state enterprises through privatisation, financial-sector reform, current-account currency convertibility, fiscal prudence, and programmes intended to accelerate growth. These reforms received support from the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, and Paris Club creditors. The resulting economic system, together with a falling budget deficit and declining debt as shares of GDP, has contributed to resilience in the face of shocks during the early 2020s, although the country has also carried a large external debt.

The economy draws on agricultural resources, phosphate minerals, tourism, and fish and seafood sales. Services account for more than half of GDP, while industry represents about one quarter and agriculture about 11.1%. By another broad measure, industry and mining together contribute roughly one-third of annual GDP. Tourism, telecommunications, and textiles have been high-growth sectors, while tourism revenue, workers’ remittances, and fish and seafood sales provide important foreign income.

Economic performance remains sensitive to external and environmental conditions. Morocco’s semi-arid climate makes agricultural output and overall GDP growth dependent on weather, with good or poor rainfall affecting results. Phosphate-price fluctuations also matter, as do the high costs associated with petroleum imports. Morocco is not an energy-producing economy and depends on imported petroleum, leaving growth exposed to commodity shocks as well as climate and pandemic shocks.

Annual GDP growth has been put at 4.13%, with per-capita GDP growth of 2.33%, though these outcomes vary with weather, sectoral performance, foreign-income flows, energy costs, commodity conditions, and reform. This performance places Morocco as the sixth-largest African economy by purchasing-power-parity GDP and gives it the seventh-highest GDP in the Arab world. Its mineral base also makes it the world’s third-largest phosphate producer.

The sectoral structure has not eliminated persistent labor-market difficulties. Structural unemployment remains a feature of the economy, the youth unemployment rate stands at 21.9%, and informal employment accounts for about 80% of jobs. Thus, Morocco combines expanding service and industrial sectors and substantial natural and tourism resources with continuing exposure to rainfall, imported-energy costs, commodity movements, macroeconomic shocks, debt pressures, and uneven employment outcomes.
