Climate adaptation and mitigation investment can shape how cities respond to environmental pressures. In the built environment, these investments may support greening construction, broader sustainable construction practices, and weatherization. They can also influence transportation-efficiency improvements and land-use strategies such as agricultural land redistribution and reforestation. Construction and transportation strategies are closely tied to emissions from construction, transportation, goods, and cement, which contribute to greenhouse-gas and global-warming emissions and to the wider environmental impact of human activity.

These climate-impact contributors are associated with climate change, global warming, and the urban heat-island effect. Such phenomena are linked to sea-level rise, flooding, erosion, and changes in weather, as well as public-health consequences such as heat illness. They also intersect with urban environmental problems including air pollution, water scarcity, subsidence, and aquifer depletion. Local vulnerability matters: high-density development and locations along rivers or coasts affect cities’ exposure to climate-related hazards.

Climate pressures can also be accompanied by social disruption, including climate conflict and the displacement of climate refugees. These disruptions, together with land-use choices, relate to changing patterns of urbanization: projections that 68 percent of the population will be urban by 2050, the growth of megacities and secondary cities, and the continuing expansion of urban areas. As these patterns develop, cities may face greater infrastructure stress and larger concentrations of impoverished populations, making climate policy, land use, construction, and transportation integral parts of the wider urban challenge.
