Permafrost consists of continuously frozen soil overlain by an active layer that freezes and thaws seasonally. As global warming, ecosystem heating, and continued warming progression alter this thermal regime, frozen ground can thaw and become warmed soil. These changes affect not only the landscape but also stored carbon, contaminants, microorganisms, infrastructure, and the wider climate.

Cold conditions suppress decomposition, allowing dead biomass to accumulate and carbon to remain stored in tundra soils. After thawing, decomposition resumes and can accelerate the permafrost carbon cycle. This process releases carbon dioxide and methane, making thawing permafrost an additional source of greenhouse gases alongside human-caused emissions, deforestation emissions, and the annual emissions of large countries. The resulting releases affect the global carbon budget and contribute to climate-change feedback, with runaway warming representing a more extreme possible outcome. Thaw processes remain uncertain, however, and greenhouse-gas releases are difficult to predict; there is broader agreement around projections of smaller emissions than around the most extreme outcomes.

Ground ice and hydrological conditions add to this uncertainty. Areas with high ground-ice content can undergo substantial ground shifting or outright collapse as thaw progresses, while conditions in which hydraulic saturation is exceeded are also associated with carbon dioxide and methane releases. Buildings and infrastructure constructed on frozen permafrost are therefore exposed to physical damage. Terrain instability can create a risk of infrastructure collapse by 2050 and costs reaching tens of billions of dollars.

Thawing ground can also expose toxic-waste sites embedded in permafrost and natural mercury deposits. Leakage from these sources can produce toxic contamination, mercury releases, and broader environmental pollution, with potential consequences for public health. At the same time, pathogenic microorganisms may survive thawing, and pathogens preserved in permafrost may revive. Such hazards have prompted concern about future pandemics, although the disease risk is generally regarded as low. Together, carbon release, unstable terrain, pollution, and biological hazards make permafrost thaw a climate issue with environmental, economic, and public-health dimensions.
