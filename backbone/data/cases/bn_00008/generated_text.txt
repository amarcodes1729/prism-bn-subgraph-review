The COVID-19 pandemic and resulting coronavirus outbreak produced severe economic fallout, prompting a series of federal relief measures. These included the Coronavirus Preparedness and Response Supplemental Appropriations Act, the Families First Coronavirus Response Act, the CARES Act, and the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021. The scale of the response exceeded that of the $831 billion American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, which had addressed the Great Recession.

The largest measure was the CARES Act, a roughly $2 trillion bill commonly described as a $2.2 trillion stimulus, equivalent to about 10 percent of U.S. gross domestic product. Its legislative procedure used H.R. 748 as a shell bill: the existing content was replaced and the measure renamed, allowing it to comply with the Origination Clause. Following bipartisan negotiations, it passed the Senate unanimously, received a voice vote in the House, and was signed by the president.

Federal relief was directed toward individual taxpayers, unemployed workers, small businesses, corporations, and state and local governments. Assistance to individuals included one-time direct cash payments to Americans, with payments of $1,200 to most single adults and larger amounts for families with children. Labor support included increased unemployment benefits and unemployment compensation, while sick-leave compensation formed another part of the broader response.

For businesses, the legislation provided Paycheck Protection Program forgivable loans and other Small Business Administration loans. Financial assistance also included corporate lending and loans to major industries. State and local governments received federal aid, while legal protections included an eviction moratorium and bankruptcy protections. Public-health measures supplied funding for vaccine research and vaccine development.

Later legislation included approximately $900 billion in additional relief through the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021. Across the response, the extraordinary fiscal scale affected federal budgets in both 2020 and 2021, with an estimated $1.7 trillion increase in the deficit associated with the largest stimulus effort.
