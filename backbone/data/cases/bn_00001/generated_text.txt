Unemployment insurance is a form of social insurance that provides partial wage replacement during unemployment. Although the program was created through federal action, benefits are generally administered and paid by the states. Wisconsin created an early unemployment insurance program, and programs were later established nationwide; the Supreme Court upheld the system’s constitutionality.

The division of responsibility between federal and state governments produces substantial variation. State eligibility requirements differ, as do definitions of “good cause” for leaving a job and penalties for misconduct. Rules and administrative arrangements vary across jurisdictions including Wisconsin, Massachusetts, Mississippi, Alaska, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania.

Eligibility generally depends on both unemployment and the circumstances of job separation. A person unemployed through no fault of their own may qualify, while someone terminated for cause, fired for misconduct, or quitting without good cause may be denied benefits. Quitting with good cause can lead to a different result under the applicable state definition. Depending on state rules and the circumstances, a claim may be completely invalidated or the claimant may instead be temporarily disqualified.

Misconduct can involve behavior in the workplace or outside it. Outside-workplace conduct, including a problematic social-media post or the commission of a crime, may affect eligibility, but the consequences depend on state law and the relevant misconduct penalty. Thus, similar conduct does not necessarily produce the same eligibility outcome everywhere.

Funding also differs across the system. Unemployment benefits may be supported through state employer payroll taxes, federal employer payroll taxes, or employee contributions. Jurisdiction, funding arrangements, and the wage-replacement function are associated with differing benefit levels. Maximum weekly payments can vary widely, with figures such as $1,015 and $235 illustrating the range among state programs.

Unemployment benefits have also been classified as social-welfare benefits, while federal tax treatment includes them in gross income. This combination of federal creation and oversight, state administration, varied financing, and state-specific eligibility standards defines the American unemployment insurance system.
