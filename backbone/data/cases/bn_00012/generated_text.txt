The Indus River rises in the Tibet Autonomous Region from a mountain spring in a trans-Himalayan setting. Its water supply is sustained by glacier meltwater and by rivers descending from the Himalayas, Karakoram and Hindu Kush. From its headwaters, the river flows northwest through a narrow Himalayan valley before making a sharp left bend after passing Nanga Parbat. It then runs generally south-by-southwest, eventually bifurcating before entering the Arabian Sea near Karachi and forming a large lower-course delta.

The river’s course is closely associated with the tectonic development of the region. The Indus–Yarlung suture zone marks the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates, dated to the Early Eocene. The resulting rise of the Himalayas was accompanied by continual diversion of river courses. In this mountain system, the Indus defines the western limit of the Himalayas, while the Yarlung Tsangpo defines their eastern limit.

Numerous tributaries contribute to the Indus and help determine its scale and landforms. The Zanskar River joins from the left in the upper reaches, while major right-bank tributaries include the Shyok, Gilgit, Kabul, Kurram and Gomal rivers. Farther downstream, the Punjab river system brings together the Chenab, Jhelum, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej rivers. Their combined waters reach the Indus through the Panjnad River, a major left-bank tributary, and are associated with the formation of the Punjab region.

The Indus is about 3,180 kilometres long and drains approximately 1,120,000 square kilometres. Its annual flow is around 175 cubic kilometres, placing it among the world’s top 50 rivers by average annual flow. This hydrological scale, maintained by its mountain-fed water supply and extensive tributary network, supports environments ranging from temperate forests and broad plains to arid countryside.
