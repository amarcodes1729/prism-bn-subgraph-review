Luddism and its modern successor, neo-Luddism, describe resistance to technology and concern about its effects on individuals, communities, economies, and the environment. The historical Luddites were active from 1811 to 1817 in the context of industrialization, while neo-Luddites respond chiefly to modern and newly introduced technologies and to claims that continuing technological improvement is necessarily beneficial.

Neo-Luddism is commonly associated with opposition to modern technology, distrust of technological improvement, concern over technology’s impacts, and, in some versions, a desire to return to more primitive forms of technology. These positions can lead to calls for reduced technology use, the safe adoption of selected technologies, or a broader return to a primitive technological level. Related outlooks include technophobia, anti-globalization, anarcho-primitivism, radical environmentalism, and deep ecology, although each places different emphasis on technological risks and desired outcomes.

Perceived risks help shape these ideological responses. Economic implications are especially associated with neo-Luddism, while concern about individual effects may also align with technophobia. Community impacts, environmental harm, and unknown effects can likewise encourage resistance, particularly where new or modern technologies have not been shown to be safe. Such concerns support precautionary approaches to adoption, including the precautionary principle and demands for proof of safety before a technology is accepted.

Resistance takes several forms. Historical Luddites are most strongly associated with technology sabotage, whereas neo-Luddites may protest technologies considered harmful, advocate simple living, abandon certain technologies passively, or, less often, engage in sabotage. People described as technophobic are more often associated with passive abandonment or avoidance. These practices may be carried out within a leaderless movement or by non-affiliated groups rather than through a single central organization. In this way, technological stance, group identity, and organizational form all influence whether resistance appears as personal withdrawal, public protest, simple-living advocacy, or direct interference with technology.
