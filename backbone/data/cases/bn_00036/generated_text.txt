Luddite resistance spans the historical Luddites of 1811 to 1817 and modern neo-Luddites or people described as technophobic. The earlier period is associated primarily with industrialization, whereas the modern era places the debate around modern, improving, or newly introduced technology. Contemporary activity often takes the form of a leaderless movement or a collection of non-affiliated groups rather than a single organization.

Different technological contexts give prominence to different perceived risks. Industrialization is associated especially with concern about economic implications, while modern technology can raise questions about its effects on individuals and communities. New or supposedly improving technologies may also provoke concern about environmental harm or unknown effects. These concerns can support a precautionary principle or a demand for proof of safety before a technology is adopted.

Neo-Luddism is the principal ideology associated with concerns about technology’s economic, individual, community, environmental, and uncertain consequences, but it overlaps with several distinct alternatives. These include technophobia, anti-globalization, anarcho-primitivism, radical environmentalism, and deep ecology. The resulting aims vary by ideology: they may involve reducing technology use, ensuring that adoption is safe, or returning to a more primitive level of technology. Likewise, the stance may range from opposition to modern technology and distrust of technological improvement to a desired return to primitive technology or a broader concern about technological impacts.

The forms of resistance reflect both the people involved and the way they are organized. Historical Luddites are associated most strongly with technology sabotage, while neo-Luddites are more often linked to passive abandonment of technology or protest against technologies considered harmful. Technophobic people may also favor simple living or withdrawal from technology. Leaderless movements and non-affiliated groups can pursue passive abandonment, public protest, simple-living advocacy, or sabotage, with the chosen practice also shaped by whether the underlying position emphasizes opposition, distrust, primitivism, or concern about technology’s consequences.
