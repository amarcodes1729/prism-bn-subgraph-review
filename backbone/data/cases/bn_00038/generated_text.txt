Externalities can arise from several kinds of spillover sources, including an independent external event, an economic activity, increased trade, a market failure to clear, or the generation of information. Their manifestations may be undesirable, as with odors from a rendering plant, or beneficial, as with the beauty and scent of a flower garden.

Information can itself have spillover effects. Greater availability of information about a person, or about people related to that person, may be associated with such externalities while also shaping whether information asymmetry is eliminated or persists. Information about related people is more strongly associated with eliminating the asymmetry than information about the person alone, although persistent asymmetry remains a possible outcome in either case. Thus, information generation links the broader issue of spillovers with the distribution of knowledge among those affected.
