The origins of the Six-Day War lay in a longer deterioration of Arab-Israeli relations. The foundation of Israel, the unresolved Palestinian refugee issue and Israel’s participation in the Suez Crisis all figured in continuing Arab nationalist hostility toward Israel and in grave threats against the Jewish population. This hostile climate was accompanied by border clashes, including Syrian fire on an Israeli tractor and a prewar aerial clash, before open war began.

The immediate crisis also involved Soviet misinformation about Israeli intentions. Against that background, the status of United Nations peacekeepers in Sinai became a central issue: their stationing there gave way to their removal, often characterized as an expulsion. This development was followed by maritime restrictions described variously as a blockade of the Straits of Tiran, the Gulf of Aqaba or Israeli access to the Red Sea.

The border confrontations and blockade contributed to rising tension and the danger of an unwanted escalation. As the crisis intensified, the Egyptian and Israeli armies mobilized, producing mutual military readiness and a growing sense that war had become inevitable. Israel then launched a surprise strike, which has also been characterized as an anticipatory attack or an act of self-defense.

Interpretations of responsibility remain contested. One view blames Egypt for the war, while another holds all sides responsible for the escalation. Israel’s opening attack is likewise described either as legitimate self-defense or as an action that did not meet that standard. These competing judgments reflect the importance assigned to the preceding clashes, mobilizations, removal of peacekeepers and blockade in explaining the outbreak of the Six-Day War.
