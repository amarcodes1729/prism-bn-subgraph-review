Digital and analog sound recording differ not only in how they preserve audio, but also in the factors that govern their performance. Both can be assessed in terms of bandwidth, signal-to-noise ratio, linearity, noise level, and distortion level, although the determinants of those qualities vary with the recording technique.

In analog recording, bandwidth depends on the capabilities of the physical and electronic circuits involved. Analog noise can arise from flicker noise or imperfections in the recording medium. These sources affect overall audio performance and may be heard as noise or broader sound distortion.

Digital recording is governed by a different set of constraints. Its bandwidth is determined by the sample rate and corresponding Nyquist frequency, while its signal-to-noise performance depends on bit depth and noise introduced by conversion circuitry. Digital systems can also encounter errors such as aliasing, artifacts from anti-aliasing filters, jitter, and quantization noise. Such effects may produce recording errors, sound distortion, or noise.

Some audible characteristics are specific to particular recording systems rather than to the analog–digital distinction alone. Transparent filtering algorithms, harmonic saturation, and speed variation can each shape perceived performance, including linearity, noise, distortion, bandwidth, and signal-to-noise ratio. The quality of a recording therefore reflects both the underlying technique and the particular circuits, media, conversion processes, and system-specific features used to implement it.
