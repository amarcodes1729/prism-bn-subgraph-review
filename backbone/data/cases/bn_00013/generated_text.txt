Hantaviruses represent a significant public-health concern in Latin America and the Caribbean. These viruses, including the Andes virus, are associated with infected rodents, particularly members of the Cricetidae family. Human infection most often follows contact with infected rodents, although human-to-human transmission is also a recognized pathway, especially in relation to Andes virus.

Regional ecological conditions influence the context in which transmission occurs. High biodiversity and altered environmental conditions are associated with the geographic setting, while climate change—including shifts in rainfall and temperature and more extreme weather patterns—can alter those conditions. Such ecological changes may amplify rodent population dynamics or increase rodent populations, affecting opportunities for people to encounter infected animals.

Exposure through these pathways can lead to hantavirus infection and, in severe cases, hantavirus pulmonary syndrome. The resulting disease burden can create a significant public-health risk and may develop into a hantavirus outbreak. Climate, ecology, rodent populations, viral agents, and routes of transmission are therefore closely connected elements in understanding hantavirus risk across Latin America and the Caribbean.
