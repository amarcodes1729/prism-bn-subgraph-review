Climate change presents intertwined environmental, economic and social risks for rural and coastal communities, particularly smallholder farmers and those dependent on rain-fed agriculture. High greenhouse-gas emissions and black-carbon emissions are associated with rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, drought, intensified hurricanes and sea-level rise. These hazards threaten water resources, agriculture, forestry and coastal zones, while also exposing affected populations directly to greater vulnerability.

Agricultural consequences include impacts on maize, coffee and other rain-fed crops. Such losses are closely linked to economic insecurity and can compound adverse health effects, human migration and broader vulnerability. Drought and changing rainfall patterns are especially connected with threats to agriculture and rain-fed crops, while intensified hurricanes and rising seas bear heavily on coastal zones and communities. Climate hazards also contribute to biodiversity loss, increased extinction risk and the listing of species as critically endangered.

The energy system remains central to the emissions outlook. Wind and solar generation and wider renewable-electricity expansion offer alternatives to fossil-fuel dependence, while continued reliance on fossil fuels is associated with high emissions and obstructed progress toward net zero. Policy regression, renewed emphasis on fossil fuels and the dismantling of climate-related institutions can reinforce that dependence and hinder emissions reductions.

Climate policy combines legislation, updates to the national climate-change strategy, adaptation and mitigation measures, and institutional support. Its stated emissions goals include a 35 percent reduction in greenhouse gases by 2030, rising to 40 percent with external support. Black-carbon targets call for a 51 percent reduction by 2030, or 70 percent with additional support, alongside a goal of net-zero emissions by 2050. International climate support therefore helps determine the ambition of the conditional targets, while those targets guide the policy measures needed to expand renewable electricity and address climate risks.

Political leadership can push policy in opposing directions. New presidential climate promises may support legislation, strategic updates, adaptation, mitigation and stronger institutions. By contrast, a fossil-fuel policy emphasis or the dismantling of climate institutions is associated with fewer climate interventions and with setbacks that sustain fossil-fuel dependence. The resulting policy direction shapes whether the energy transition advances, emissions decline and the mounting risks to ecosystems, livelihoods and vulnerable communities are reduced.
