Digital audio technology represents sound through digital signals, commonly using pulse-code modulation and numerical sampling. A typical specification uses 44,100 samples per second with 16-bit resolution. By contrast, analog audio technology represents sound through an analog electrical signal; in either system, the chain ultimately relates an audio signal to the sound wave that is heard.

An analog-to-digital converter performs analog-to-digital conversion, translating an analog electrical signal into digital form. Pulse-code modulation or numerical sampling provides the encoding method for this conversion. The reverse operation is handled by a digital-to-analog converter, which performs digital-to-analog conversion so that recorded information can enter a playback chain involving an audio playback machine, an audio power amplifier, and a loudspeaker.

Once sound is digitally represented, compression, storage, processing, and transmission components support its convenient manipulation, storage, transmission, and retrieval. These capabilities in turn facilitate recording, editing, modifying, copying, and playback. Computers, audio playback machines, and other digital tools can take part in those operations.

The form of the signal also bears on copying quality. Analog copying is associated with generation loss and signal-quality degradation, whereas copying a digital signal is strongly associated with no degradation in signal quality. This ability to preserve copies, together with convenient processing and handling, distinguishes digital audio workflows from their analog alternatives.
