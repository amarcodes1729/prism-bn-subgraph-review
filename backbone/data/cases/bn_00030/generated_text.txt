Just war theory is commonly organized around three pillars: jus ad bellum, jus in bello, and jus post bellum. Together, they address the justice of resorting to war, conduct during war, and the pursuit of justice after war. These areas distinguish the right to use force and the causes for war from actions taken in war and obligations arising after hostilities.

Jus ad bellum concerns whether force is initiated under just or unjust conditions. Its central principles include just cause, legitimate authority, and proportionality. Jus in bello shifts the focus to actions in war, particularly proportionality and the distinction between combatants and noncombatants. Jus post bellum addresses justice after war and the principles that should govern the post-war period.

The applicable just-war principles therefore depend on the area being regulated. Questions about initiating force invoke standards such as just cause and legitimate authority; questions about wartime conduct invoke proportionality and distinction; and questions concerning the aftermath of conflict invoke post-war justice.
